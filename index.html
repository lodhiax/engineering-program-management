Medical Devices
Decoupling integration from development to clear an FDA V&V submission
The problem

A closed-loop surgical instrument simulator was stalling because integration work was sequenced to wait on full subsystem completion. With hardware, firmware, and software workstreams advancing at different rates, integration was perpetually last and always late. The stakes were more than schedule — the simulator's output was the V&V data earmarked for FDA submission.

What moved it

The WBS was redesigned to give the integration workstream independent entry points at each development phase, enabling parallel validation against partial builds. Engineering report release cycles were tightened so that design outputs, verification evidence, and technical documentation were produced and approved in phase with milestones rather than assembled after the fact.

Outcome

Integration bottlenecks were eliminated as a source of schedule risk. The simulator validation completed on cycle and produced the V&V data package that supported the FDA submission directly. The WBS structure and release governance model became the repeatable framework the engineering team carried into subsequent programs.

Medical Devices
Requalifying a critical component to take $45 out of every instrument built
The problem

A precision instrument carried a single-source, high-cost purchased component — a needle assembly — that set both its material cost and its supply risk. There was no qualified alternative and no measurement baseline proving where assembly variation actually came from, and a production ramp was approaching that would multiply every dollar of that cost across the batch.

What moved it

A Lean Six Sigma DMAIC project mapped the end-to-end assembly workflow, and a Gauge R&R study established a trustworthy measurement baseline before any change was proposed — instrumentation before optimization. An alternate supplier was evaluated against the full performance and quality profile, then qualified through bench testing and process validation (IQ, OQ, PQ) so the switch rested on evidence rather than price alone.

Outcome

The qualified alternate met every performance and quality requirement while removing roughly $45 of material cost per instrument — on the order of $500K in annual savings at production volume — with no loss of performance or reliability. The requalification also standardized the assembly process and reduced single-source supply risk.

Functional Safety
Turning a fault into a safe state before it could reach a clinical reading
The problem

Field feedback surfaced a quiet but serious risk: after certain fault conditions, the device could keep operating and display readings a clinician might trust, with no clear signal that the measurement could no longer be relied on. In a regulated clinical setting, a device running in an unknown state is a patient-safety hazard, not just a defect.

What moved it

The response was a fail-safe Sleep Mode driven from Voice-of-Customer input through to verification. On detecting a predefined fault, the device stops acquiring or displaying measurements, raises a clear alert, and requires a reset plus passing self-tests before it can return to clinical use. Delivering it meant threading new requirements through hardware power-state transitions, firmware fault-detection and recovery logic, and the system risk file at once, with traceability held from customer need to test.

Outcome

The feature closed the failure path by design — a faulted device can no longer present a reading as valid. Fault detection, user alerts, and mandatory self-test-before-return were verified through functional, fault-injection, and regression testing, and the work fed directly into the FDA 510(k) documentation package.

Regulatory
Rebuilding a submission's traceability to clear an FDA inquiry
The problem

An FDA review returned observations against the software interface that displayed and managed a regulated device's data. The gaps were not in the product's behavior but in its evidence: incomplete requirements traceability, verification records that no longer fully supported the performance claims, and design documentation scattered across teams and years.

What moved it

Each observation was triaged into documentation, verification, traceability, and performance-evidence categories, then worked to closure. The core effort was reconstructing the Requirements Traceability Matrix end to end — mapping user needs to requirements to design outputs to verification and validation — and generating new records where history was missing. Where prior test evidence fell short, targeted retesting and reanalysis were coordinated to substantiate every claim in the package.

Outcome

The submission went back with a complete, defensible traceability chain and verification evidence that stood on its own. Documentation and DHF quality improved in the process, and the response gave Regulatory Affairs a technically robust package aligned to the agency's expectations.

Design control artifacts

📄
Design Input
System Requirements Document
Defines the system-level functional and performance requirements the device must meet. Captures stakeholder needs, intended use, user needs, and design inputs per 21 CFR Part 820.30(c) and ISO 13485 design control requirements.
The foundation of the design control process. Every downstream design output, test, and verification activity must trace back to this document.
SRD
💻
Design Input
Software Requirements Document
Specifies software functional and non-functional requirements per IEC 62304. Defines software items, interfaces, safety classifications, and performance criteria. Serves as the binding input for software architecture design and unit-level testing.
Drives the IEC 62304 software development lifecycle from architecture through unit and integration testing. Auditors expect this to be traceable in both directions.
SRD-SW
📋
Traceability
Requirements Traceability Matrix
Bidirectional traceability linking user needs through system requirements, software requirements, design outputs, verification tests, and validation evidence. The single artifact that demonstrates completeness of the design control chain to FDA and notified bodies.
Critical for 510(k) and PMA submissions. Auditors inspect this first to confirm every requirement is tested and every test is justified by a requirement.
RTM
🌟
Phase Gate
Phase Gate Exit Review
Formal design review deck presented at each phase gate — Concept, Feasibility, Development, Verification, Validation, and Transfer. Documents exit criteria status, open actions, risk posture, and the go or no-go recommendation per 21 CFR 820.30(e).
The governance checkpoint that keeps design programs on track. No phase advances without resolved open items and documented approval from the review board.
Gate Review
⚠️
Risk Management
Risk Management File
End-to-end risk management documentation per ISO 14971 — covering hazard identification, probability and severity scoring, risk controls, residual risk evaluation, and benefit-risk analysis. Maintained as a living file throughout the product lifecycle.
Required for all medical device submissions. Demonstrates systematic harm prevention and that residual risk has been evaluated and accepted appropriately.
RMF
✅
Verification
Verification and Validation Plan
Defines the V&V strategy, test methods, acceptance criteria, responsible parties, and schedule for confirming design outputs meet design inputs — verification — and that the finished device meets user needs — validation — per 21 CFR 820.30(f) and (g).
Keeps verification and validation clearly separated. Conflating the two is one of the most common FDA audit findings in software-intensive device programs.
V&V Plan
🔨
Architecture
Software Architecture Document
Describes software architecture, system interfaces, data flows, module decomposition, and design decisions for regulated software per IEC 62304 Section 5.3. Maps software items to safety classifications and defines the integration strategy across subsystems.
The technical backbone of the software design file. Required by IEC 62304 and reviewed in software audits by FDA and notified bodies to confirm architectural rigor.
SAD
🐛
Defect Management
Anomaly and Defect Tracking Log
Structured log of software anomalies and defects identified during verification and validation, including severity classification, root cause, resolution, and regression test status. Required by IEC 62304 Section 5.6 for software problem resolution.
Demonstrates controlled handling of defects throughout the development lifecycle. Particularly critical for Class B and Class C software items under IEC 62304.
Anomaly Log
🛡️
CAPA
CAPA Report
Corrective and Preventive Action documentation covering problem identification, root cause analysis using fishbone, 5-Why, or fault tree methods, action plan, effectiveness verification, and formal closure. Required by 21 CFR 820.100 and ISO 13485 Section 8.5.
One of the most scrutinized records in any FDA inspection. A well-structured CAPA with demonstrated effectiveness check signals a mature, functioning quality management system.
CAPA
📁
Design History
Design History File Index
Master index of the Design History File cataloguing all design control records — user needs, design inputs, design outputs, design reviews, verification, validation, and design transfer documents — organized per 21 CFR 820.30(j).
An FDA investigator reviews this first. A clean, complete, well-organized DHF index signals a controlled design process before a single underlying document is opened.
DHF Index
🔄
Design Transfer
Design Transfer Plan
Documents the transition of the design from R&D to manufacturing — covering production procedures, Device Master Record readiness, manufacturing process validation, training, and transfer acceptance criteria per 21 CFR 820.30(h).
Often the most underdocumented phase of a device program. A robust transfer plan prevents the post-market quality escapes and production nonconformances that trace back to incomplete handoffs from engineering.
